How much should a contractor website cost in Wyoming?

We will not list prices here. What a contractor website should cost depends on what the site has to do: help a busy customer understand your work, see where you work, and call you.

What actually drives the cost

Owner-operators ask us this all the time, and it is a fair question. A website for a Wyoming contractor is not a poster. It is the place a homeowner or a facilities manager lands after a Google search, often on a phone, while they are trying to get a problem handled. The work that goes into that site — and therefore what it should cost — follows the job it has to do.

We do not put prices on this page. Pricing is a conversation about your trade, the towns you cover, and the pages you actually need. What we can do here is explain the pieces that drive the work, so you can judge a proposal in plain English.

How many pages the site needs

A single brochure page can look finished and still leave a customer guessing. A useful contractor site usually needs a clear home page, a way to reach you, and enough room to explain the work you actually sell. That is not decoration. Each real service you offer, and each town you truly work in, is a page a customer might land on from search. More honest pages means more writing, more photos, and more structure. That is a real driver of cost. Inventing pages for towns you do not serve is not.

Service pages and town pages

If you do roofs, remodels, and concrete, those are different searches. If you work Riverton, Lander, and the rest of Fremont County, those are different searches too. We build contractor websites around the services you sell and the towns you cover, and we treat Fremont County as a real market — see our Fremont County, Riverton, and Lander pages for how that looks in practice. Skipping those pages makes a site cheaper to launch. It also hides you from the people who are ready to call.

Copy, photos, hosting, and tracking

Copywriting in plain English

Someone standing in a driveway does not want a brand essay. They want to know what you do, where you work, and how to reach you. Writing that clearly — and making it honest about your service area — takes time. Generic filler text is faster to publish and easier to ignore. Good copy is part of what you are paying for, whether we write it with you or you already have it ready.

Photos of real work

Stock mountains and a stock handshake do not help a customer trust a local contractor. Photos of your actual jobs, crews, and finished work do. Taking them, picking them, and placing them on the right pages is part of the job. A cheap site often skips this and looks like it could belong to anyone, anywhere.

Hosting and maintenance

Launch is not the finish line. A site has to stay updated, backed up, and usable on a phone. Forms have to keep working. If you add a service or a town later, the site should be able to say so. That ongoing care is part of the real cost of a website, and it is the work we describe on our website maintenance page. A bargain launch with no plan after the fact is how sites go quiet and then go stale.

Tracking so you know what is working

If you cannot tell whether a call came from the website, you are guessing. Call tracking, a form that actually finishes, and a number that is easy to tap are part of building a site around leads, not around looking busy. That is the point of our lead generation work. Tracking does not have to be complicated. It does have to exist.

What a cheap site misses — and how to judge one

The lowest quote is often low because the site is missing the parts that create calls. We see the same gaps over and over:

  • No tap-to-call button. The number is in the footer, baked into a picture, or missing on mobile. A busy customer will not hunt for it. See why hiding the phone number kills leads.
  • No town pages. The site never names Riverton, Lander, or the other places you actually work, so local searches have nothing solid to grab onto.
  • No tracking. You cannot tell which calls came from the site, so you cannot tell whether the site is doing its job.
  • No clear services. Everything is lumped into a vague “we do it all” paragraph, and the customer is left to guess.

Judge a website by the calls it brings in

A contractor website should be judged the same way you judge a new tool: does it do the work? Pretty is fine. Pretty without a way to call is a brochure. When we look at a site, we ask a simple set of questions. Can someone on a phone tell what you do in a few seconds? Can they see the towns you cover? Can they tap to call without hunting? After launch, are conversations actually starting?

If the answer is no, the site is not cheap. It is unfinished, even if the invoice was small. If the answer is yes, you are paying for the right things — structure, copy, photos, care after launch, and a path to the phone. That is the standard we use in our website design work, and it is the standard we will use on a free estimate call with you.
